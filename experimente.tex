\section{Experimental Evaluation of Different Output Models}
\label{sec:experiments}

This chapter presents the experimental evaluation of the proposed models. It discusses the specific characteristics of each model, followed by the evaluation metrics and visual comparisons. The hyperparameter-tuning process used to identify a well-performing configuration for a selected combination of output formulation and loss function is then described. This is followed by a broader comparison of models using different output formulations and loss functions. Finally, the best-performing model is evaluated on a previously unseen test dataset.

All experiments were conducted on a computing cluster using one NVIDIA H100 GPU per training run. The initial training runs completed the full 200 epochs and took approximately 30 hours with a batch size of 10. Early stopping was introduced for subsequent experiments; all of these runs terminated before reaching 100 epochs, substantially reducing their training time.

The models were trained using the AdamW optimizer with a weight decay of \(10^{-8}\). The training and validation sets were generated using a fixed random split with seed 0, ensuring identical data partitions across experiments. The split contains $72{,}000$ training samples and $8{,}000$ validation samples. The constant values are listed in table~\ref{tab:training_configuration}. Some values, especially the last two were changed during hyperparameter training. This is made clear when describing the models.

\subsubsection{Permutation-Invariant L1 Loss}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.99\textwidth]{diagrams/twooutputs/twooutput__two-output-L1__bad_02__sample_073451__thesis-comparison.pdf}
    \caption[Two-output L1 failure case]{Failure case for the two-output L1 model. The prediction lacks the structure of the target spot and does not recover a plausible foreground mask.}
    \label{fig:twooutput-l1-bad-example}
\end{figure}


The first two-output baseline uses a permutation-invariant L1 loss on the two predicted spot images. In Table~\ref{tab:two_output_models}, the L1 variants now form the strongest two-output group for recognition and intensity reconstruction. The standard L1 model reaches Dice $0.9156$, \gls{rmse} $0.0510$ / $0.0109$, soft Dice$_{\mathrm{whole}}$ $0.9327$, and recognition $0.9323$. Increasing the batch size to $100$ gives the best recognition among the two-output models, $0.9370$, and the lowest whole-image \gls{rmse}, $0.0096$, while Dice remains high at $0.9222$. The L1 objective therefore no longer appears as a weak separation baseline in the aggregate metrics; instead, it provides a strong intensity-preserving two-output solution, although the selected failure case in Figure~\ref{fig:twooutput-l1-bad-example} shows that individual difficult samples can still fail qualitatively, since the two-output approach is relatively obvious and the L\_1 Loss is very well knows as well as this beeing one of the first training tries. This model will be considered the baseline for future experiments

The additional L1 hyperparameter curves are shown in Figure~\ref{appfig:twooutputmissingloss}. They indicate that the L1 settings converge to competitive validation metrics, with the batch-size-$100$ run slightly ahead of the standard L1 run in Dice, whole-image \gls{rmse}, soft Dice$_{\mathrm{whole}}$, and recognition. The model with only $32$ base channels remains the weakest L1 variant: Dice decreases to $0.8784$, \gls{rmse} rises to $0.0648$ / $0.0146$, soft Dice$_{\mathrm{whole}}$ falls to $0.9009$, and recognition decreases to $0.9068$. This suggests that reduced capacity mainly harms both shape agreement and intensity fidelity, even though the model remains substantially stronger than the older metric values suggested. Representative dense comparison sheets in the appendix material illustrate the remaining qualitative failure modes.

\subsubsection{Composite Reconstruction Loss}

The composite reconstruction loss still performs best on the foreground \gls{rmse} among the two-output models in Table~\ref{tab:two_output_models}, with $0.0407$, and it also gives the best soft Dice$_{\mathrm{whole}}$, $0.9515$. These values show that the reconstruction term is effective at preserving the measured intensity distribution.

Its remaining weakness is relative rather than absolute: compared with the two L1 models, Dice decreases to $0.8800$ and recognition to $0.9197$. Thus, the reconstruction objective is excellent for intensity agreement, but it is slightly less reliable for assigning the two separated objects than the L1 objective. The selected comparison examples support this interpretation and show that the model remains sensitive to dataset morphology and overlap geometry.


\subsubsection{Weighted Tversky Foreground Loss}

    \label{fig:twooutput-tversky-problem}
\end{figure}


\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{diagrams/twooutputs/tversky/twooutput_tversky_loss_components.pdf}
    \caption[Two-output Tversky loss components]{Loss components for the two-output Tversky model. The mask component converges more reliably than the intensity component.}
    \label{fig:twooutputloss-components}
\end{figure}


The weighted Tversky foreground loss changes the trade-off of the two-output model substantially. It gives the highest Dice in Table~\ref{tab:two_output_models}, $0.9627$, but it is not the strongest model for intensity reconstruction or recognition. Its recognition is only $0.6481$, and its \gls{rmse} values, $0.6664$ / $0.1544$, are much worse than the L1 and reconstruction objectives. The result indicates that foreground-aware supervision produces very strong mask overlap, but in this two-output formulation it does not by itself preserve the separated intensities or consistently recover both spots.

An initial Tversky run was trained on a smaller dataset containing approximately $4{,}000$ samples. This dataset still contained repeated source spots, with individual spots appearing up to three times. The loss curves in Figure~\ref{appfig:loss-small-dataset} show that the training loss decreased, but the model did not reach the performance of the full-dataset Tversky model. As shown in Table~\ref{tab:two_output_models}, the small-dataset model reaches Dice $0.7293$, \gls{rmse} $0.5783$ / $0.1553$, soft Dice$_{\mathrm{whole}}$ $0.4926$, and recognition $0.5755$. These values are clearly below the full-dataset Tversky model for Dice and recognition and remain far below the L1 models for intensity-oriented metrics. The validation data followed the same split strategy as the other experiments and did not contain source spots used during training. The performance gap therefore indicates that the limited dataset size and repeated spot appearances restricted generalization, even though the loss curves themselves appeared stable.

However, the Tversky model has the worst \gls{rmse}, $0.6664$ / $0.1544$, and the lowest soft Dice$_{\mathrm{whole}}$, $0.5341$. In other words, it achieves very strong foreground overlap but its separated intensity values are much less accurate, and this also lowers the recognition score compared with the L1 and reconstruction models. Figure~\ref{fig:twooutputloss-components} shows the same pattern during training: the mask-related component converges more cleanly than the intensity component. The problem case in Figure~\ref{fig:twooutput-tversky-problem} demonstrates the resulting failure mode, where the foreground mask remains plausible while the assigned intensity distribution is unstable. This can be explained by the intensity term having only a weight of $0.2$ in the loss function\footnote{The Tversky loss was later adapted for the multi-output model, where it produced the strongest model in this thesis.}.

The per-dataset results in Figure~\ref{fig:twooutput-per-dataset} are important for interpreting the table. The aggregate values are not caused by uniform performance on all materials. Instead, the models show a strong dependence on the source dataset: easier datasets with compact, well-localized spots dominate the good examples, whereas more difficult datasets with larger deformation, stronger overlap, or less regular spot morphology dominate the failure cases. This pattern is also visible in the selected examples: high-performing cases for the L1 and reconstruction objectives are mostly taken from the IN718 twins dataset, while many of the poor cases come from Ti7Al, Iron, Iron deformed, or Al big grains. The spot-count visualization in Figure~\ref{fig:twooutput-spot-counter} reinforces this point. A model can achieve moderate average spot recognition while still frequently recovering only one of the two spots, or none, on difficult samples. For this reason, the batch-size-$100$ L1 model is the strongest two-output candidate when the priority is reliable spot recognition and intensity-resolved separation, while the Tversky model is mainly useful when foreground overlap is prioritized over intensity fidelity.

Table~\ref{tab:one_output_models} shows that the Dice/L1 model is the stronger one-output model overall. It reaches Dice $0.6843$, \gls{rmse} $0.1350$ / $0.0337$, soft Dice$_{\mathrm{whole}}$ $0.7997$, and recognition $0.7747$. The per-dataset performance differs substantially as well, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:oneoutput-dataset-heatmap}. This wide spread is the central weakness of the one-output formulation.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{diagrams/oneoutput/oneoutput_problem.pdf}
    \caption[One-output problem case]{Problem case for the one-output model. The residual construction cannot preserve shared overlap intensity reliably, which leads to missing or distorted signal in the second spot.}
    \label{fig:oneoutputpreviewexample}
\end{figure}

The main limitation of this model is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:oneoutputpreviewexample}. The directly predicted spot captures parts of the target morphology, but its spatial extent is underestimated. The second output, constructed as the residual image, deviates more strongly from the target because intensity removed for the first spot is no longer available for the second one. This is especially problematic in the overlap region, where the measured intensity is the sum of both physical spots. The network would need to assign shared intensity to both separated outputs, but the residual formulation only allows the intensity to remain in one channel.

The Tversky model changes the metric profile, but it still does not truly separate the two spots. Instead, the qualitative examples in the supplementary handout indicate that it tends to crop out one plausible spot while leaving the residual output incomplete. Compared with the Dice/L1 model, Dice decreases from $0.6843$ to $0.6291$, \gls{rmse} worsens from $0.1350$ / $0.0337$ to $0.1652$ / $0.0390$, soft Dice$_{\mathrm{whole}}$ drops from $0.7997$ to $0.6314$, and recognition decreases from $0.7747$ to $0.7319$. Thus, the Tversky loss sacrifices intensity fidelity and does not make the residual representation more robust in the aggregate metrics.

The spot-counter plot in Figure~\ref{fig:oneoutput-spot-counter} is important for interpreting these averages. A moderate spot-recognition value does not mean that both spots are recovered consistently. Instead, the model often predicts one plausible spot while the residual channel remains incomplete or distorted. This explains why the one-output Dice/L1 model can look competitive in individual good examples while still failing on difficult samples.

    \caption[One-output per-dataset metrics]{Per-dataset metrics for the one-output models. The heatmap shows that the residual formulation is particularly unstable on difficult datasets with strong overlap, deformation, or irregular spot morphology.}

The per-dataset heatmap in Figure~\ref{fig:oneoutput-dataset-heatmap} shows that this behavior is not uniform across the data. The best selected Dice/L1 cases are concentrated in the IN718 twins dataset, where the model can reconstruct compact examples with high Dice and spot recognition. The weak Dice/L1 cases are mainly from Ti7Al and Iron. For the Tversky model, strong examples occur across Ti7Al, Al, IN718 twins, Al big grains, and Al small grains, but the worst cases include IN718 twins, Al deformed LoG, Ti7Al, and Iron. This distribution indicates that the residual formulation is sensitive to both overlap geometry and source-dataset morphology. It can work on favorable cases, but it does not generalize reliably when the two spot contributions are strongly entangled.

Overall, the one-output approach is useful as a baseline but remains physically limited. The Dice/L1 objective gives the better balance of spot recognition and intensity accuracy, while the Tversky objective worsens all metrics shown in Table~\ref{tab:one_output_models}. Compared with the two-output models, the one-output formulation avoids channel-assignment ambiguity, but it introduces a stronger representational bottleneck: the second spot is not independently predicted and therefore cannot retain intensity that has already been assigned to the first output.
